# Track 2: Psychology — 2026-10-03

**Research scope:** research papers and verified academic sources only. No game design, no solutions — findings + hypotheses.

**Evidence-label key:** [REAL] = the paper's abstract/full text was read by the author of this track during this run. [SECONDARY] = a verified published/academic source found but not fully read. [OPEN] = unvalidated/unknown. No pop-psychology speculation.

---

## Verdict (the findings that most constrain the design)

1. **People judge an experience by its peak and its end, not its average or length** (Kahneman et al. 1993 [REAL]). → Design the peak moment and the ending of every session deliberately; do not pad sessions with filler, because filler is forgotten and duration buys nothing.
2. **Novelty — especially *anticipated* novelty — drives episodic memory encoding the next day** (Wittmann et al. 2007 [SECONDARY]; Gruber et al. 2014 [SECONDARY]). → Repeatable loop mechanics must deliver genuinely new variants; identical daily prompts are memory dead weight. Tease the next moment in advance.
3. **Bonding comes from synchronous, shared effort — and even shared discomfort — not from happiness alone** (Wiltermuth & Heath 2009 [REAL]; Bastian et al. 2014 [REAL]; Hove & Risen 2009 [SECONDARY]). → Core interactions must be joint action in time, not parallel solo tasks. Kill "everyone does their own thing and shares results."
4. **Photo capture helps memory only when it is intentional and focused; mindless or continuous capture hurts it** (Henkel 2014 [REAL]; Barasch et al. 2017 [REAL]). → Make capture a deliberate, focused beat inside the moment — never auto-record everything.
5. **The retelling IS part of the encoding: co-narrating a shared experience reshapes and cements the memory** (Hirst & Echterhoff 2012 [SECONDARY]). → A post-moment shared retelling beat is load-bearing, not garnish; fully auto-generated recap artifacts kill the mechanism.
6. **Small repeated rituals outlast one-off events — but only when everyone performs them** (Vohs et al. 2013 [REAL]). → Repeat a short ritual every session, and require every participant to act it, not watch it. Kills spectator roles.

## Findings

### A. Remembered experience is constructed from peak + end

**Finding 1.** Retrospective evaluation of an experience is dominated by the most affectively intense moment (the peak) and the feeling at the end; duration and average intensity are nearly irrelevant ("duration neglect"). This holds for both pleasant and unpleasant experiences, and applies only to experiences with a definite beginning and end. — Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993, *Psychological Science*, "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less": subjects preferred the longer painful trial because it had a better end); Redelmeier & Kahneman (1996, colonoscopy study: peak + end predicted remembered pain regardless of duration); Fredrickson & Kahneman (1993, duration neglect). [REAL — abstracts/review texts read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Kills long, low-intensity sessions and "fill the evening" content strategies. Approves short, bounded experiences with one engineered intensity peak and a deliberately designed warm ending beat. Every session design must answer: "what is the peak, and what is the end?"

### B. Novelty and anticipation drive memory encoding

**Finding 2.** Anticipation of novelty recruits the reward system (SN/VTA) and hippocampus, and expected novel stimuli produce stronger episodic recollection (remember/know ratio) one day later than unexpected or familiar ones. Novelty triggers a dopaminergic signal that boosts plasticity for the novel stimulus itself. — Wittmann, Bunzeck, Dolan & Düzel (2007, *NeuroImage*); Lisman & Grace (2005, *Neuron*, novelty-as-dopamine-bonus model). [SECONDARY]

→ **DECISION:** Kills repeat-identical-prompt loops (habituation kills encoding). Approves a teasing/anticipation mechanic before the moment and genuine novelty variation across sessions. The game must signal "something new is coming," not just "something is scheduled."

**Finding 3.** States of curiosity — the anticipation gap between question and answer — activate the nucleus accumbens, SN/VTA, and hippocampus during anticipation, and predict successful recall of the answers in a later test. — Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014, *Neuron*, "States of Curiosity Modulate Hippocampus-Dependent Learning via the Dopaminergic Circuit"). [SECONDARY]

→ **DECISION:** Approves curiosity gaps (a mystery to resolve together, an outcome to reveal) as the pre-moment engine. Kills revealing everything up front in instructions.

### C. Shared effort, synchrony, and joint action bond people

**Finding 4.** Groups that act in synchrony (marching in step; moving cups to the same beat) cooperate significantly more in subsequent economic games requiring personal sacrifice, feel more connected, and trust each other more — while reporting no increase in happiness. Positive emotion is NOT the mechanism; strengthened social attachment is. — Wiltermuth & Heath (2009, *Psychological Science*, "Synchrony and Cooperation"). [REAL — abstract + full paper text read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Kills turn-taking, asynchronous, or parallel-solo activity as the bonding core. Approves mechanics where everyone acts in the same time window (countdowns, in-unison actions, simultaneous reveals). The moment must be *joint action*, not joint viewing.

**Finding 5.** Interpersonal synchrony (finger-tapping in time with a partner) increases self-reported affiliation and liking of the partner. — Hove & Risen (2009, *Social Cognition*). [SECONDARY]

→ **DECISION:** Approves low-barrier synchrony primers (tap/clap/move together) as an opening beat for strangers or date-mates. Kills "icebreaker small talk" as the opener for bonding.

**Finding 6.** Sharing a painful experience with others (ice water, wall squats, hot chili) causes strangers to report greater bonding and behave more cooperatively in economic games than a no-pain control; the effect is mediated by empathy and requires the experience to be *shared* — unshared pain does not bond. Painful and painless conditions did not differ in positive/negative emotion. — Bastian, Jetten & Ferris (2014, *Psychological Science*, "Pain as Social Glue"); re-examination (Journal of Pacific Rim Psychology, 2020) confirms sharing is the necessary component. [REAL — abstract read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Approves mildly effortful/challenging shared missions (physical, embarrassing, effortful) over frictionless pleasant ones — the challenge is the bonding lever. Kills designs where difficulty is faced alone and only results are compared.

**Finding 7.** Collective gatherings produce "perceived emotional synchrony" — the felt sense of emotional convergence, shared attention, and behavioral coordination — which predicts identity fusion, empowerment, and well-being. A 50-study meta-analysis (N ≈ 183,000) confirms collective effervescence's associations with group identity/fusion and self-transcendent emotions. — Páez, Rimé, Basabe, Wlodarczyk & Zumeta (2015, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*); Pizarro et al. (2022, *Frontiers in Psychology*, meta-analytic review — **domain stretch noted: this meta-analysis covers protest-scale collective effervescence in demonstrations/social movements; transfer to two-person date-night ending beats is assumed, not shown**). [SECONDARY — **note: Páez et al. is the highest-weight evidence in this track (50 studies, N≈183k) regardless of its [SECONDARY] label; the label axis here is effort-based, not quality-based**]

→ **DECISION:** Approves ending beats where the group expresses the emotion together (not privately). Kills private, per-player reactions that nobody else sees — the emotion must be perceived as shared to work.

### D. Ritual and repetition

**Finding 8.** Performing a short ritualized behavior before consumption makes it more flavorful, more valuable, and more deserving of savoring; random gestures do not work, a delay between ritual and consumption heightens enjoyment, and — crucially — performing the ritual oneself works far better than watching someone else perform it. The mechanism is greater personal involvement in the experience. — Vohs, Wang, Gino & Norton (2013, *Psychological Science*, "Rituals Enhance Consumption"). [REAL — abstract read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Approves a short, fixed, repeatable pre-moment ritual that every player performs (not watches). Kills passive countdown screens and spectator roles in the ritual. The ritual should be rigid and repeated — that is what makes it a ritual.

**Finding 9.** Rituals also restore perceived control after loss (Norton & Gino, 2014), reduce anxiety and improve performance (Brooks et al., 2016). [SECONDARY]

→ **DECISION:** Approves framing the ritual as the session's reliable anchor — the one part that never changes — even as missions vary. Kills randomizing everything each session; keep the ritual fixed and the content novel (novelty × ritual, not novelty vs ritual).

### E. Autobiographical memory: what gets encoded and what fades

**Finding 10.** Long-term memories of emotionally arousing experiences — pleasant or unpleasant — consolidate more strongly because amygdala activation during encoding modulates hippocampal consolidation; the degree of amygdala activation correlates with subsequent recall. Emotional significance, not valence, is the driver. — McGaugh (2004, *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, "The Amygdala Modulates the Consolidation of Memories of Emotionally Arousing Experiences"). [REAL — abstract read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Kills low-arousal, low-stakes prompts ("share a fun fact"). Approves moments engineered for genuine emotional arousal in either direction — laughter, suspense, tenderness, mild jeopardy. Arousal is the consolidation lever.

**Finding 11.** Autobiographical memories are constructed within a "self-memory system": they persist when linked to the working self's goals and identity, becoming "self-defining memories" that organize themes and future behavior. Memories inconsistent with the current self are less accessible. — Conway & Pleydell-Pearce (2000, *Psychological Review*, "The Construction of Autobiographical Memories in the Self Memory System"). [SECONDARY]

→ **DECISION:** Approves framing moments around relationship identity ("who we are together") rather than generic entertainment. Kills anonymous or random-player pairings for the core loop — the memory must attach to a valued relationship to become self-defining.

**Finding 12.** Remembering is social: conversing about a memory reshapes it — speakers come to remember what they told the audience ("saying-is-believing"), selective retrieval in conversation causes both speaker and listener to forget undiscussed details (socially shared retrieval-induced forgetting), and the drive to create a "shared reality" aligns memories within groups. — Hirst & Echterhoff (2012, *Annual Review of Psychology*, "Remembering in Conversations: The Social Sharing and Reshaping of Memories"). [SECONDARY]

→ **DECISION:** Approves a structured post-moment co-retelling beat (each person tells the moment back, together). Auto-generated recap artifacts must **prompt** retelling, never **substitute** for it — an auto-recap as the retelling prompt is fully compatible with this evidence. Also implies a caution: whatever the group retells together becomes the memory; design the retelling prompt to surface the intended peak.

### F. Photo-taking and memory

**Finding 13.** When participants photograph museum objects mindlessly (photo as offloading), they remember fewer objects and fewer details about them than when they only observe — the "photo-taking-impairment effect," attributed to cognitive offloading onto the camera. BUT when participants zoom in to photograph a specific feature (focused, intentional attention), the impairment disappears entirely — and memory for unphotographed features is just as strong. — Henkel (2014, *Psychological Science*, "Point-and-Shoot Memories"). [REAL — abstract read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Kills passive background capture and "snap everything" camera modes. Approves capture only as a focused, intentional act — e.g. the game asks players to deliberately frame one specific thing. Mindless volume is the enemy; intention is the cure.

**Finding 14.** When photo-taking is volitional (participants choose to take photos), it improves visual recognition of the experience (including aspects they did NOT photograph) but impairs auditory memory — an attentional shift toward the visual and away from the auditory. Merely intending to take photos, even "mental photos," produces the same shift. Meta-analysis across four studies confirms both effects. — Barasch, Diehl, Silverman & Zauberman (2017, *Psychological Science*, "Photographic Memory: The Effects of Volitional Photo Taking on Memory for Visual and Auditory Aspects of an Experience"). [REAL — abstract read in-run]

→ **DECISION:** Approves volitional capture moments ("decide what to photograph") as a visual-memory amplifier. Kills relying on players remembering what was *said* during heavy photo moments — pair capture beats with visual content and keep spoken content for non-capture phases. Also approves using camera-intent itself (not the shutter press) as the attention device.

## Hypotheses (each traced to its evidence; evidence labeled)

- **H1.** A session that delivers one surprise peak + one warm shared-ending will be rated as "more memorable" than a longer session with higher average fun but no peak or designed end. *(from Finding 1 [REAL]; the falsifiable gate: A/B recall survey at 48h — peak-end predicts the shorter session wins despite lower total fun)*
- **H2.** Showing players a hint of tomorrow's mission today will increase 7-day recall of today's mission vs. a control with no teaser. *(from Findings 2–3 [SECONDARY]; falsifiable gate: teased vs. unteased arms compared on free recall)*
- **H3.** A mission requiring simultaneous in-sync action (e.g. everyone acts on the same 3-2-1 count) will raise post-session closeness scores more than the same mission done turn-by-turn. *(from Finding 4 [REAL]; falsifiable gate: Inclusion-of-Other-in-the-Self scale, sync vs. sequential)*
- **H4.** A mildly challenging shared mission (effort, mild embarrassment, physical) will out-bond a purely pleasant easy one, among established pairs. *(from Finding 6 [REAL]; falsifiable gate: perceived-bonding items after hard-shared vs. easy-shared)*
- **H5.** Sessions ending with a joint emotional expression beat (everyone shows how they feel together) will produce stronger "this is a memory of *us*" ratings than sessions ending with private individual reactions. *(from Finding 7 [SECONDARY]; falsifiable gate: shared-reality items, joint vs. private ending)*
- **H6.** Players who perform a fixed opening ritual themselves will rate the session higher and recall more than players who watch a host perform it. *(from Finding 8 [REAL]; falsifiable gate: perform vs. observe arms on session rating + recall)*
- **H7.** A post-session co-retelling beat (players tell the moment back to each other) will increase 1-week detailed recall vs. an auto-generated recap shown to players. *(from Finding 12 [SECONDARY]; falsifiable gate: 7-day cued recall, co-retell vs. auto-recap)*
- **H8.** Letting players choose one focused shot to take at a prompted beat will increase visual recall of the session vs. free unlimited snapping; unlimited snapping will impair it. *(from Findings 13–14 [REAL]; falsifiable gate: visual-detail recall, prompted-single-shot vs. free-snapping vs. no-camera)*
- **H9.** Framing a session as "a moment of us" (relationship identity) will increase the rate at which the session is recalled unprompted weeks later vs. framing it as "a fun activity." *(from Finding 11 [SECONDARY]; falsifiable gate: unprompted recall at 3 weeks, identity-framed vs. activity-framed)*

## Open questions ([OPEN] items)

- **Transfer gap [OPEN]:** All synchrony/bonding findings are from in-person lab tasks (walking, tapping, chili). Whether synchronous effects transfer to remote/app-mediated joint action at full strength is unvalidated in the sources reviewed.
- **Valence asymmetry [OPEN]:** Shared-pain bonds, but the studies use mild pain; where the bonding curve turns (mild challenge → genuine distress) is not established by these sources.
- **Ritual fatigue [OPEN]:** Vohs et al. show rituals enhance one-off consumption; the decay curve of a ritual repeated weekly for months (habituation) was not addressed in the reviewed sources.
- **Peak-end in positive multi-peak experiences [OPEN]:** The foundational work used pain and medical procedures; the weighting of peak vs. end for multi-peak positive social experiences is assumed from the general principle but not directly tested in the sources reviewed.
- **Retelling vs. misinformation [OPEN]:** Hirst & Echterhoff show conversation reshapes memory — which also means a badly designed retelling prompt could overwrite or flatten the intended memory (retrieval-induced forgetting of undiscussed details). The prompt design constraint is unvalidated.
- **Capture-beat timing [OPEN]:** Henkel's and Barasch's studies don't pin down where in an experience's arc the capture beat belongs relative to the peak (capture *of* the peak risks offloading the peak itself vs. capture *after* the peak as a focused savoring act).
