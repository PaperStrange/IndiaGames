# Player record — Anchorhead (Thirteenth Bell competitor B2 — SUBSTITUTE for Outer Wilds)
**Filed:** 2026-10-20 · **Source:** live browser play via Parchment at pr-if.org/play/anchorhead (Release 5 / Serial 990206; by Michael Gentry), first-person player voice
**Role in the matrix:** substitute for Outer Wilds (B2), a paid native install. Anchorhead preserves B2's core structure exactly: you progress by *learning* — a strange town and a decaying house open up only as names, records, and histories are cross-referenced into understanding. No stats, no grind: knowledge is the keyring.
**What the substitute leaves unanswered:** Outer Wilds' loop is spatial and time-boxed (a 22-minute solar system, piloting, dying and re-entering with what you know). Anchorhead is a fixed three-day text world explored by typed commands. The knowledge-gating is the same; the vehicle and the clock are not. Recorded as the gap.
**Coverage:** DEEP PARTIAL — two full in-game days played across several hundred moves, score 9 points; the third day (the comet's arrival) and any ending were not reached. Blind: no walkthroughs, guides, hints, or maps.
**Evidence:** 4 full-page PDF captures banked in `captures/` of the filed package, text-verified by the coordinator (Anchorhead renders as web text in Parchment, so the captures are legible transcripts of actual play, including the deepest capture's full-day scrollback).

## The session (shape, not transcript)
November 1997. I play the wife of Michael, who has inherited a decaying ancestral mansion in the town of Anchorhead. My first errand — collecting the house keys from the real-estate agent — fails in the game's first knowledge beat: the office is deserted, and the answering machine offers one whispered word: **"Verlac."**

That word became the session's emblem. Verlacs are the family branch Michael descends from, and the file room's cabinets answer only to a specific name. Looking up "Verlac" showed the property file had been deliberately cleaned out — but the house and cellar keys were hidden in the back of the folder (+2 points). One overheard word, converted into physical access to the entire game world: the purest knowledge-as-progress beat of the session, and it kept recurring. The family album and courthouse archives answer only by name; dark rooms required the kitchen flashlight; Michael's palmed faculty card, shown to the university librarian, opened the restricted collection (+2).

The central mystery I assembled purely by cross-referencing records: Michael's own bookmark note ("born-died same date? have to chk. records") sent me to the courthouse birth/death archives, where a chain verified itself — every Verlac heir across three centuries is born on the day his predecessor dies. One entity, returning through the bloodline. The pattern implies an heir born March 14, 1997 — the day Edward Verlac died — and I never identified that heir. Edward's hidden journal (found under a cupboard lining, +2) gave a safe combination (20-22-18), word of a protective "amulet," and an unsolved hint that the cellar entrance "is in their names"; the day-two newspaper corrected the public story — Edward murdered his wife and daughters and died by suicide in an asylum in March 1997 — while child abductions, one every six months, have continued without him.

My deepest point came from acting on a dream: night one showed Michael handing me a golden sphere, saying he didn't know what it opened. In the Study, turning a matching brass sphere on the mantel slid the fireplace aside into a spy corridor — peepholes into the children's bedroom and through a portrait's eyes — ending in a hidden Observatory, where the charts show the cult's prophesied "great event," a comet, arrives tomorrow: day three.

**Exact stop point:** the Study, day two, evening. Michael has vanished, leaving his laptop asking for a 4-digit passcode ("An important date, maybe?"). I tried two principled candidates — both ACCESS DENIED — and stopped rather than brute-force. Score 9. Unsolved at the stop: the laptop and Michael's whereabouts, the wine-cellar entrance, the safe and amulet, the 1997 heir, the locked attic, the church-cellar padlock, the metal door under the bridge, the telescope's rectangular slot — with the comet due tomorrow.

## The matrix question (B2): what does knowledge-as-progression feel like from the seat?
Like being handed a town that only answers specific questions. Every system in Anchorhead — cabinets, archives, the librarian, the house itself — refuses generic curiosity and rewards the exact name, date, or object you earned elsewhere. The genealogy chain was the peak: five separate lookups, none meaningful alone, snapping into a single horrifying sentence. That is Outer Wilds' "the log is the level" in prose form.
