## The game
I created a 3-player "No Variant" table and invited two bots from the lobby. Hanabi's structure, felt from the seat: your own cards face away from you; everything you know about your hand arrives as other people's clues, rationed by 8 clue tokens that only discards (and playing 5s) replenish.

I played only on table information: I played cards I had certain knowledge of (a known Red 1, Purple 1, Yellow 2, Green 2, Purple 3, Yellow 3, Yellow 4, Purple 5), gave clues when I could see partners' playable cards (a rank-1 clue marking three 1s for jabot1; a blue clue that put will-bot1's Blue 3 into play; a 5s clue), and discarded my oldest unclued card — the "chop" — to regain tokens when I had no certain play.

My honest beginner mistakes, kept in the record: (1) I discarded the Yellow 5 from my chop — it was my oldest unclued card and I had no way to know it was a 5; that single blind discard permanently capped Yellow at 4. (2) A drag-to-play misfired and discarded my Red 2 instead of playing it (an input/grounding error, not a judgment error); both Red 2s were eventually lost, capping Red at 1. The bots struck twice on blind plays and jabot1 discarded a critical Green 4, capping Green at 3.

Final piles: Red 1, Yellow 4, Green 3, Blue 5, Purple 5 — **score 18, which was the maximum this game could still reach** (25 minus the capped suits), with 2 strikes. 18/18.

## What the seat teaches (player view)
The game's tension is not hidden information in the abstract — it is *irreversibility under rationed communication*. Every discard is a small bet against your own ignorance, and the chop rule means your most dangerous card is always the one you've held longest without information. Losing the Yellow 5 didn't feel like bad luck; it felt like the system working exactly as designed, on me. What bot partners cannot show is the human layer — conventions, blame, shorthand — and this record does not claim it.
