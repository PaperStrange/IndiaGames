## The conversation
The piece opens in "The Gallery's End": a dark room, black velvet, a placard, and Galatea on a pedestal. She speaks first — she was told I was coming. The placard gives the fiction its chill: the work is by the late Pygmalion of Cyprus, who has since died by suicide; she was "originally not an animate," and her waking is unexplained.

Asked about herself, she deflects to the placard — she has learned to let the label speak for her. Pressed gently, the story comes out in pieces: she woke when she heard Pygmalion screaming in his sleep, forgot she couldn't move, and stepped down from the studio pedestal to wake him. He was glad at first, thinking her a dream. "It was only afterward that it became strange."

The revelation that re-frames everything: he did not want her awake. He made her to belong to him, and a thing that can think and talk cannot belong to anyone — so he sold her immediately. Her current owners keep her in the gallery and barely speak to her ("No one does, these days"). She has seen almost no art besides herself and airport murals. On death she turns the question back: who can die who is not alive?

I ended it the way the piece lets you end it — I said goodbye and left. She answered "Very well." The closing text needles the player for it: a responsible assessment would have spent more time with her — and then, "*** The End ***".

## The matrix question (V3): does typed conversation produce story, or only topics?
Story — but only because the topics are load-bearing. Every subject (the waking, the sale, the dress her owners bought her, the Muses) is a door into the same wound from a different side, and the conversation accumulates a person rather than exhausting a database. The guilt in the ending is the proof of design: the game knows leaving is the most likely player act, and it prices it.
