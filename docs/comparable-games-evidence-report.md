## A. Signature distinctives per game

1. **Outer Wilds** — Knowledge *is* the progression. A 22-minute loop wipes everything except what you learned; no gear upgrades, no XP. The Ship Log offers two views: **Location view** (map-based, "where have I been") and **Rumour view** (causal graph, "what connects to what"). Player evidence (ggapp.io review): *"the only thing that changes is your knowledge of how it works"*; players specifically praise ship-log categorization.
2. **The Forgotten City** — The **Golden Rule**: one person's sin kills everyone and resets the loop, turning morality into a game mechanic. Solved setup is compressed via **Galerius task delegation** and retained inventory/knowledge. AGDA judges: *"Each new bit of information about the world is presented through natural-feeling dialogue with NPCs and is revealed in a way that doesn't overload you with data all at once."*
3. **Twelve Minutes** — A single tiny top-down apartment; learned information unlocks new dialogue options and physically compresses setup. Time is shown on **diegetic clocks**, not a HUD timer.
4. **Majora's Mask** — 20+ NPCs each run a visible 3-day schedule; the **Bomber's Notebook persists across loops**, recording schedules, accomplishments, and alarms. Real-time ~54 minutes per 72-hour cycle; songs manipulate time (slow, skip, reset).
5. **Deathloop** — 4 districts × 4 time periods = **16 world variants**; "Visionary Leads" converge into one planned "perfect day"; **Infusion** keeps chosen gear across loops. Anti-distinctive: waypoint-heavy Leads can over-solve the deduction (see C).
6. **Return of the Obra Dinn** — The **journal is the game**: record identity, cause, responsibility for 60 crew; validation is **delayed/batched** (confirmations arrive in chapters, not per-click). ResetEra player: *"It's the 'FIGURE IT OUT. Ain't gonna say shit' feeling."*
7. **Pentiment** — The entire UI *is* a manuscript: journal, map, glossary rendered as a book the protagonist draws in; typography and page turns are world-building. Reviewer antonsalogvinov (9/10): *"Визуал потрясяющий, журнал заданий, карта и глоссарий сделаны в виде книги"* ("The visuals are stunning; the quest journal, map and glossary are made as a book").
8. **Heaven's Vault** — Discoveries become **timeline nodes filterable by age, location, character**; translation guesses feed back into the narrative and the game never confirms correctness. Player garbeige (10/10): *"feel like a genius when a translation comes together while also subtly supporting you towards that conclusion."*

## B. UX patterns worth stealing (with source games)

1. **Two-view journal: Place × Theory** (Outer Wilds, Heaven's Vault). One view organizes clues by *where* (map/district), the other by *how they connect* (causal web/timeline). For the tabletop demo: an Anchor Board with a "Place view" (6 districts) and a "Theory view" (thread strings between clues).
2. **The book as the central deduction surface** (Obra Dinn, Pentiment). One physical artifact players keep returning to, with delayed validation instead of instant feedback. Verbatim praise: Obra Dinn players call it *"the best deductive game I've ever played"* (RPG Maker Web forums); Pentiment's Pipironni (10/10): *"the glossary, always at hand, makes everything clear and accessible."* **Design implication:** the demo's journal must be a single handsome booklet, not loose handouts.
3. **Persistent NPC × phase schedule matrix** (Majora's Mask). The 3DS remake added a quest tracker + more forgiving saves, and GamesRadar notes these QoL features as the reason the remake *"introduced"* the game to a wider audience. A visible schedule grid lets players plan *before* acting — the foundation for the prediction verb.
4. **Never cover the map** (Majora's Mask QoL, Descent: Legends of the Dark precedent). Dicebreaker: Descent's companion app *"expands and remixes"* physical components rather than replacing tactile play. For the demo: **side inspector/drawer for encounters; full modals only for loop-ending commitments (Anchoring)**. Map stays visible at all times.
5. **Drip-feed information through natural dialogue** (Forgotten City). The AGDA-winning property: new information arrives via NPC conversation, never as data dumps. For tabletop: NPC cards reveal information in *conversation beats*, not bullet lists.
6. **Diegetic or atmospheric time display** (Twelve Minutes, Majora's Mask). Twelve Minutes puts time on wall clocks; Majora's Mask shows a persistent clock and changes Clock Town's music per day (IGN noted the quickened Day-2 tempo conveys time pressure). For the demo: a **central clock** with phase lighting, ambient shifts, and a short reset transition — atmosphere *and* state feedback.
7. **State compression after mastery** (Forgotten City, Majora's Mask 3D). Solved introductions shorten; mastered setup is delegated or skipped. Players explicitly credit this QoL when it exists.

## C. Anti-patterns to avoid

1. **Uncompressed repetition → walkthrough bypass.** Twelve Minutes players: oalpaslan (7/10): *"The repetitive nature of the game made me watch a walkthrough and be done with it."*; Neqmera (8/10): *"the gameplay becomes very repetitive after a while, and in this case, it starts to get boring"*; johkonut (6/10): *"You'll find yourself skipping through voice lines for 90% of the game."* **Rule: no beat may repeat identically more than twice; mastered content auto-compresses.**
2. **Quiz-like single hidden paths with obscure triggers.** Twelve Minutes GLaDOS (2/10): *"This is only a puzzle game if your definition of puzzle is randomly mashing pieces together until you happen upon a combination that fit"*; GemsExplorer (8/10): *"some triggers don't make sense"*; TrueOptimist (4/10): *"things you need to do to proceed are sometimes extremely obscure."* **Rule: every required deduction must have ≥2 discoverable paths.**
3. **Over-solved waypoints that replace deduction.** Deathloop KillerTeddyB3ar (7/10): *"an overly handholdy time loop… If the player needed to actually identify their targets through clues and deduction, not by following a quest marker… I'd probably love it more."* Adamflame7's praise (*"writing down everything for you… easy to follow"*) shows the same system is *convenient* — the failure is removing the deduction, not the notebook. **Rule: the journal records; it never points.**
4. **Half-working trackers are worse than none.** Forgotten City Samvell (4/10): *"The journal… lets you put a tracker on a quest… except half the time this tracker doesn't work and you have to navigate on your own… it just creates confusion."* **Rule: every tracking affordance in the demo must work 100% of the time or be cut.**
5. **Dialogue density without gameplay variety.** Forgotten City PenetratorGod (5/10): *"Just walking around, listening to dialogue, and then looking around for notes makes the game boring."*; angelknives (8/10): *"The mystery is intriguing enough to carry the game, even through some of the more tedious parts."* **Rule: each phase must contain at least one mechanical verb beyond read/choose.**
6. **Traversal padding between puzzles.** Heaven's Vault HydrophobikFish (8/10): *"the best space-archeologist game… BUT the game's painfully slow walking speed and sometimes-awkward camera controls really limit the down time in between language puzzles."* For tabletop: no "walk to the location and read a paragraph" without a decision attached.
7. **Endgame power/momentum collapse.** Deathloop GoatGuy (8/10): *"your final loop… will in all likelihood be laughably easy… the last few hours drag as you finish up the handful of things still worth doing."* **Rule: the Midnight phase must escalate stakes, not mop up.**
8. **Forced full replays for alternate endings.** Twelve Minutes Gyan_fg (6/10, on seeing other endings): *"si quieres ver otro final tienes que empezar el juego desde cero… tuve que ver una guía"* ("if you want to see another ending you have to start the game from scratch… I had to watch a guide"). For the demo's single session: the final choice must be reachable from accumulated knowledge, not from replaying.

## D. Ranked top-five recommendations (each tied to player evidence)

1. **Ship the journal as two views: Place × Theory.** *Evidence:* Outer Wilds players single out the Ship Log's dual views; its 95% Overwhelmingly Positive across ~106k reviews with the loop's only persistent system being *knowledge organization*. *"The only thing that changes is your knowledge of how it works."* **Apply:** Anchor Board = district map view + string-and-pin theory view. This directly answers "hard to imagine just by texts" — the theory view makes thinking *visible*.
2. **Make the NPC × phase schedule matrix the planning surface — and add the prediction verb.** *Evidence:* Majora's Mask players credit the Bomber's Notebook for making a 20-character schedule tractable (GameFAQs player review: *"As you relive the same three days over and over again, you gradually become familiar with the routines of various characters… there's just so much more depth to the characters in Majora's Mask"*); 6.8M copies sold and a 2022 NSO re-release show the pattern's longevity. **Apply:** before each travel, players *predict* who/what will change; correct foresight becomes Momentum. This is the "one distinctive verb" beyond visit/read/choose.
3. **Auto-compress anything the table has mastered.** *Evidence:* Twelve Minutes' 73% (vs. Outer Wilds' 95%) is driven substantially by repetition complaints — johkonut: *"skipping through voice lines for 90% of the game… The overall UX of the game is very cumbersome"*; oalpaslan quit to a walkthrough. Conversely the 3DS Majora remake's quest tracker is credited with widening the audience. **Apply:** second visit to a district = shortened intro; delegated tasks skip mastered setup; highlight *only* what's new this phase.
4. **Delayed, batched validation — let players feel like geniuses.** *Evidence:* Obra Dinn (96%, ~35k reviews): *"It's the 'FIGURE IT OUT. Ain't gonna say shit' feeling"*; Heaven's Vault garbeige: *"feel like a genius when a translation comes together while also subtly supporting you towards that conclusion."* **Apply:** the demo never confirms a theory mid-phase; Anchor commitments resolve in batches at phase boundaries with visible consequences.
5. **Keep the digital demo complementary, never a card-for-card port.** *Evidence:* Descent: Legends of the Dark — Dicebreaker notes the companion app *"expands and remixes"* the physical game; Mansions of Madness 2e players credit the app with making setup *"near instantaneous"* and adding *"atmosphere and mystery that would be difficult to achieve via cards and paper alone."* **Apply:** the web demo handles clock, ambient transitions, and the Anchor Board state; the tabletop kit keeps the tactile journal, cards, and GM dashboard. Each medium does what it's best at.
